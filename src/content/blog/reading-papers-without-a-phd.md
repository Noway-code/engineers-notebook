For a long time I read papers like novels — first word to last, in order,
giving up around section 4. The problem wasn't the papers. It was that I was
using the only reading strategy I had, on a document that isn't written to be
read that way.

Papers are written to be *scanned first and read selectively*, by people who
already know the neighborhood. If you don't know the neighborhood, you have to
build that context deliberately instead of hoping it arrives by page six.

## Three passes

S. Keshav's [*How to Read a
Paper*](https://web.stanford.edu/class/ee384m/Handouts/HowtoReadPaper.pdf) lays
this out properly and it's two pages long. The short version I actually use:

1. **Five minutes.** Title, abstract, section headings, conclusion, and every
   figure caption. Then answer one question: what does this paper claim is
   different from what came before? If you can't answer it, that's the thing to
   chase, not the contents of section 3.
2. **An hour, no references.** Read the body, skip the proofs and the detailed
   related work, and take the evaluation seriously — what did they measure,
   against what baseline, on whose hardware. Most of a systems paper's honesty
   lives in its graphs' axes.
3. **As long as it takes.** Only for papers you're going to build on.
   Reconstruct the argument as if you were the one making it, and note every
   place you'd have made a different decision. Those notes are the actual
   output.

Most papers stop at pass one. That's not a failure — that's the filter working.

## What made it click

Two things.

The first was giving myself permission to not finish. A paper I abstract-scan
and drop still leaves me knowing it exists, which is most of the value when I
hit the problem it solves two years later.

The second was reading the related-work section *first* on anything outside my
depth. It's a free annotated bibliography written by someone who read all of it
so you don't have to, and it tells you which two of the forty citations are the
ones everybody builds on.

You don't need the credential. You need to stop reading in order.
